AP Human Geography · Topic 2.7 Population Policies

You are the population advisor.

You’ll advise two governments. First, everyone examines the United States. Then you’ll choose a second country facing a different situation and compare the two.

In each country you’ll diagnose the problem, then choose one policy per decade from 2025 to 2055. To enact a policy you must correctly identify it as pronatalist or antinatalist and explain its intent. Every choice reshapes the population pyramid.

Learning target

I can explain the intent and effects of population policies on a country’s population size and composition.

Topic 2.7 · Population Policies — pronatalist, antinatalist, and immigration policies that promote or restrict population growth.

Pronatalist policy

A government policy meant to increase the birth rate — e.g., cash for babies, paid parental leave, free childcare.

Antinatalist policy

A government policy meant to decrease the birth rate — e.g., family planning access, girls’ education, birth limits.

Total fertility rate (TFR)

The average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current birth rates.

Replacement level ≈ 2.1

The TFR needed to keep a population the same size over time, ignoring migration.

For teachers: how the model works
  • Students complete the United States first (Part 1), then choose one comparison country (Part 2). The final report puts both side by side with three comparison reflection questions.
  • Each round projects the population forward 10 years using a simplified cohort-component model (5-year age groups, male/female, age-specific survival and fertility).
  • Each policy shifts the TFR by an amount scaled to real-world evidence: cash incentives in low-fertility countries have small effects; girls’ education, ending child marriage, and contraceptive access have larger effects in high-fertility countries. Effects are cumulative and start immediately — real policies take years.
  • Every option in a country matches its goal (all pronatalist or all antinatalist); options differ in strength, so students predict which will work best and compare results.
  • Starting pyramids use approximate 2025 age structures; headline figures (births, TFR) reflect 2025 national statistics. Some countries also have an underlying fertility trend (e.g., China −0.05 per decade), and population momentum means a policy can push fertility the right way while the base still moves the other way — the results screen explains this when it happens.
  • Immigration is modeled only for the United States, held constant at about 1 million net migrants per year, mostly young adults. Students can’t change it; it’s there so the U.S. pyramid behaves realistically and to set up the comparison question about why the U.S. keeps growing with a TFR below 2.1. Other countries are modeled without migration.
  • Students finish with a report they can copy into Google Classroom or print to PDF. Nothing is saved, so they should submit before closing the tab.
